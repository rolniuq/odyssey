## The one question this page answers

"What if I always make the locally best choice and never look back — is the final answer still the
global best?" For a wide class of problems the answer is **yes**, and the reward is comedy: instead
of exploring many plans, the algorithm walks once, committing as it goes. Greedy is not a hope or a
vibe — it is a specific, **verifiable property**. This page teaches the property, shows the proof
shape, and demonstrates where the habit is a trap.

## Start with an analogy

A cashier making change from a drawer of quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies uses a simple ritual:
_take the largest coin that fits, repeat._ For 67 cents: quarter, quarter, dime, nickel, two
pennies. It is obviously optimal for these four denominations, and nobody second-guesses it.

But the habit is what is on trial, not the coins. If the drawer held only 1s, 3s, and 4s, the same
ritual breaks 6 cents as 4 + 1 + 1 — three coins — while 3 + 3 uses two. The habit was never a
general law; it was a habit with a hidden proof that happened to hold for the first coin set.

That is the whole discipline of greedy: **make a local rule, but only trust it once you can prove it
safe.** A proven habit becomes a one-pass algorithm; an unproven one is a guess wearing a costume.

## The recipe: a local rule, then a proof

Every greedy algorithm is built from two parts:

1. **A local rule** — the move you always make when you look at the current state.
2. **A safety proof (exchange argument)** — show that any optimal solution can be rearranged so its
   _first_ step is exactly your greedy first step, without losing optimality. Once that holds, the
   same argument applies to the remainder, and greedy is correct by induction.

The exchange argument is the difference between "it makes sense" and "it is correct." If you cannot
even sketch one, you do not have a greedy solution yet — you have a candidate.

## Jump Game: one number, the whole frontier

Jump Game gives you an array where `nums[i]` is the maximum jump from index `i`. Notice what the
problem does **not** ask: _which_ hops to take. It asks only _whether any_ landing path exists. That
collapse is exactly the greedy opening.

Track one integer: `reach`, the farthest index reachable from anywhere seen so far. Walk `i` left to
right; for each cell inside the frontier, extend it:

```
reach = max(reach, i + nums[i])
i more than reach  ->  stuck, return false
```

The invariant: every index at or under `reach` is reachable by _some_ path, so individual paths are
interchangeable and can be forgotten. The exchange argument: reaching farther can never make you
reach _fewer_ cells, so extending is always safe. The visualizer above replays `[2, 3, 1, 1, 4]` —
watch the marker slide to the last index.

## Can Place Flowers: verify a local window

Can Place Flowers asks whether `n` flowers fit under an adjacency ban. The local rule is immediate:
a plot is plantable if it is `0` **and** its neighbors (or the edge) are `0`. Greedy plants the
leftmost plantable plot and moves on.

Why is that safe? Planting at the earliest valid spot never hurts future capacity: any later
planting that a different scheme places at the same or further index is not blocked by a plant that
came _earlier_ than both. You can prove, step by step, that greedy stays as good as any plan — the
"greedy stays ahead" variant of the exchange argument.

## When the safe habit lies

Return to the coins. Denominations 1, 3, 4 and amount 6: greedy says three coins, truth says two.
The lesson is not "greedy is unreliable" — it is that **every greedy claim must carry its proof.**
When no exchange argument exists, the honest tool is dynamic programming: instead of committing to
one local choice, keep the best of _all_ choices at each state. Tomorrow's pages teach exactly that.

## What this is NOT

| Misconception                              | Truth                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Greedy is guessing well"                  | It is an exchange argument plus induction. Without the proof you have a heuristic, which may part ways from optimal.          |
| "Greedy always needs to look ahead"        | A correct greedy never looks ahead — the safety proof is what replaces looking, and the result is a one-pass scan.            |
| "Greedy and DP pick the same moves"        | DP evaluates all local choices per state; greedy commits to one. Greedy is an O(n) special case that is only sometimes valid. |
| "A greedy failure means the idea is wrong" | The idea — deciding locally — is fine; the local _rule_ lacked proof. Change the rule or switch to DP, not the discipline.    |

## Why this matters for the rest of the trip

Interval scheduling, Huffman coding, Dijkstra's shortest path, and the MST algorithms (Kruskal,
Prim) are all greedy ideas — each one shipped with a proof. Recognizing "this looks greedy" is
worthless until you can also answer "and why is it safe?" Teaching yourself to reach for that
question first will save you from a whole family of elegant-but-wrong submissions.

Solve the two problems above — and before coding each one, write its exchange argument in one
sentence in your head. If a sentence is impossible, the problem probably wants DP, which is the very
next page.
