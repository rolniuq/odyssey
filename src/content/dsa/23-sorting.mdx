## The one question this page answers

Sorting usually means comparisons — n log n of them — but not every sortable pile deserves that
price. This page answers one question: _when can you sort without the full cost, by leaning on
structure and invariants instead?_ Two answers: when the alphabet is tiny (0, 1, 2) the Dutch
national flag sorts in one linear pass with three pointers, and when you need only one position the
partition step of quicksort can be reused as quickselect — finding the k-th largest without ever
sorting the rest. Both are the same lesson: an invariant turns a loop that looks like it should be
expensive into a loop that provably cannot be.

## Start with an analogy

A warehouse worker sorting three boxes — red, white, blue — off a conveyor belt. Instead of emptying
the whole belt onto the floor, she keeps three zones: the red zone at the front, the blue zone at
the back, and the untouched middle. She reads one box at a time. A red box goes to the front zone; a
blue box goes to the back zone; a white box just stays put in the middle. When the middle is empty,
the belt is sorted — and no box was ever picked up twice.

The three zones are the **invariant**; the box in her hand is the **mid pointer**; the front and
back boundaries are **low** and **high**.

## The Dutch national flag invariant

The array splits into four regions at all times:

- `arr[0 .. low-1]` — all 0s, locked.
- `arr[low .. mid-1]` — all 1s, locked.
- `arr[mid .. high]` — unknown.
- `arr[high+1 .. end]` — all 2s, locked.

The loop reads one unknown cell at `mid` and shrinks the unknown region until `mid > high`:

- `arr[mid] === 0` → swap with `arr[low]`, `low++`, `mid++` (a 0 locks in front).
- `arr[mid] === 1` → `mid++` (a 1 is already home).
- `arr[mid] === 2` → swap with `arr[high]`, `high--`, but **do not** `mid++` (the swapped-in value
  is unknown and must be read next).

The visualizer runs all of this on `[2,0,2,1,1,0]`: watch the unknown middle shrink and the locked
zones grow, ending at `[0,0,1,1,2,2]`. Cost: every element is classified once and swapped at most
once — O(n) time, O(1) space.

## Quickselect: one side of the partition

The `k`-th largest does not require knowing the whole ordering. Quickselect reuses the quicksort
partition and then descends into only one side:

1. Pick a pivot (random, or median-of-three, to dodge the worst case).
2. Partition so larger values come before the pivot; the pivot lands at some index `p`.
3. If `p === k - 1`, the pivot **is** the answer. If `p > k - 1`, recurse into the left side (larger
   values); otherwise recurse into the right side.

Each level halves the amount of work done, so the average is O(n) — a strict improvement over the
O(n log n) a full sort would charge — with the caveat that a hostile pivot can degrade it to O(n²).
Your browser runner accepts the simple `sort`-then-index solution too; the point of the problem is
to feel why the partition-only version is the smarter one.

## What this is NOT

| Misconception                          | Truth                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "Sorting always costs n log n"         | The flag sort is O(n) because the tiny alphabet lets each element move at most once; n log n is the price of comparison sorts.       |
| "Quickselect returns the sorted array" | It returns one position; the array around the answer may stay unsorted — that partial promise is what buys the O(n) average.         |
| "The flag sort works for any numbers"  | It only classifies three known values; general data still needs a comparison sort.                                                   |
| "mid never stays put"                  | A 2 swap deliberately keeps mid in place so the unknown swapped-in value gets examined; skipping it is a famous bug.                 |
| "In-place means the sort is stable"    | Both flag sort and partition are in-place, but neither preserves the relative order of equal keys — stability is a separate promise. |

## Why this matters for the rest of the trip

Invariants are the quiet engine of every efficient algorithm: the two-pointer days, binary search,
and the monotonic stack all rest on "this region is settled, that region is unknown." The flag sort
is the purest version — three pointers, one loop, a promise you can state in one sentence. And
quickselect is the pattern behind "I only need the top k", showing up again in heap problems and
streaming statistics. Master the habit of writing the invariant down before the loop, and the loop
writes itself.

Solve the two problems above. Sort Colors is the invariant made visible; Kth Largest is the
partition put on a diet. For each, write the invariant (or the partition rule) on paper before you
code — and for Kth Largest, convince yourself why recursing into one side is enough.
