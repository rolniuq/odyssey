## The one question this page answers

Day 8 told you how to _compute things_ on a tree (depth, inversion) by recursing. Today the question
is the other way round: _in what order do you simply visit every node?_ The honest answer is that
there are several regimes, and the one you want is decided by the shape of the answer you are
building — deepest-runner-first (depth-first) or level-by-level (breadth-first).

## Start with an analogy

A tour of a building with many floors and many rooms. **Depth-first** is your guide rushing to the
basement before seeing the lobby's other wing: you finish one whole staircase of rooms, then climb
back and take the next. Left-to-right at each choice is just your touring policy. **Breadth-first**
is walking an orderly floor-by-floor sweep: you clear the lobby completely, then every first-floor
room, then every second-floor room — like a fire warden checking each floor's fire doors in turn.

The lobby is the root; floors are levels; your "memory of rooms still to visit" is a stack (for
depth) or a queue (for breadth).

## Depth-first: three flavors, one recursion

Depth-first means "finish an entire child before starting the sibling." Exactly when you print the
node within that recursion gives three names:

- **Preorder** — node, then left, then right. Best for copying a tree's shape.
- **Inorder** — left, node, right. On a binary search tree this prints sorted order.
- **Postorder** — left, right, node. Children before parents: the shape for deleting a tree or
  computing parent tasks from child results.

The base case is always `null` — "nothing to visit" — and the recursion itself is the stack that
remembers how to climb back.

## Breadth-first: one queue, one level at a time

Level order has a different engine: a FIFO queue instead of the call stack. Servicing one level is a
recipe with two steps, repeated:

1. Snapshot how many nodes are waiting right now — that is the width of the current level.
2. Pop exactly that many, recording their values into one level array, and push the children they
   each reveal.

Because you only push children of nodes you have popped this round, the queue always holds the next
level and nothing else — the "edge" of the traversal. The result is an array of level arrays,
`[[3], [9, 20], [15, 7]]`, watched live in the diagram above.

## Same Tree: two walkers in lockstep

"Are these two trees identical?" is not "do they hold the same stuff?" — it is "do they wear the
same shape and the same labels at every position?" The recursion walks both trees at the same spot:

1. Both `null` — equal.
2. One `null`, or their values differ — different, and no further work is needed.
3. Otherwise compare left-with-left and right-with-right.

This is why values-equal-but-shape-different trees fail: the third case carries the two walkers to
symmetrically opposite slots, where a mismatch pops instantly.

## What this is NOT

| Misconception                               | Truth                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Preorder means fast, postorder means slow" | They are same-cost visits in three orders; performance difference is the queue-vs-stack tree shape, never the label. |
| "Level order is a depth-first recursion"    | It is BFS — the queue is mandatory; recursing yields a depth-first order instead.                                    |
| "Same Tree means the values match"          | It means structure AND values at mirrored slots; [1,null,2] and [1,2] differ despite matched content.                |
| "Inorder always prints sorted output"       | Only on a binary search tree. On any other shape inorder is just a visit order, not a sort.                          |

## Why this matters for the rest of the trip

Level order is the model for the coming queues-heavy problems (serialize/deserialize, right-side
view, Zigzag level order), and inorder-on-BST is how the tree days connect back to Day 5's binary
search — same ordering principle, now structural. Meanwhile Same Tree's lockstep recursion is your
template for every "compare two trees" problem, from subtree checks to tree equality in the Kth
closest style questions.

Solve the two problems above today — they are your first taste of "the answer shape dictates the
traversal".
