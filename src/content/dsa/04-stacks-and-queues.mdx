## The one question this page answers

Some problems only make sense if you can ask "what is the _most recent_ thing I have not finished?"
— the nearest unmatched bracket, the last action to undo, the current minimum while items are being
pushed and popped. The naive tool for "most recent" is to keep re-reading the whole history. The
stack asks a sharper question: _can the newest item be the only thing touched, every time?_ Yes —
and every operation costs O(1).

## Start with an analogy

A neat pile of plates in a cafeteria. You put down a fresh plate — it lands on top. You grab a plate
— you grab the top one. You never slide plates out of the middle, and you never reach under the
pile. The _insert_ and the _remove_ are the same physical move against the same end of the pile.

The pile is the stack; the end that all the action happens at is its **top**; "the last plate I put
down is the first one I pick up" is the guarantee that makes a stack a stack.

## Last in, first out, precisely

A stack is a linear structure with three O(1) operations: **push** (place an item on top), **pop**
(remove the top item and reveal the one beneath), and often **top/peek** (read the top without
removing it). There is no "insert in the middle" and no "remove the oldest" — both would break the
invariant that only the top is reachable.

That strict rule is exactly why recursions, undo histories, and expression parsers are all stacks
under the hood: the thing you most recently set aside is the thing you are most likely to touch
next.

### Why matching forces a stack

"([)]" has perfectly balanced counts — two pairs of opened and closed. But it is invalid, because
")" pairs with the `[` on top rather than the `(` that was opened last. Only the stack knows that
the `(` was still on the pile above the `[`; a counter of opens cannot see shapes, so a counter
approves the wrong string. Whenever a problem pairs things and cares about _which_ partner each item
chose, the top-of-stack order is the only thing that captures it.

### Min Stack: remembering every depth's minimum

The trick question — "what is the current minimum?" — has an O(n)-looking answer if you scan, and a
broken answer if you cache a single number (pop the true minimum and the cache is a lie). The reason
it is O(1) is that you snapshot at every level. When a new low is pushed, remember; when something
pops, the previous level's snapshot is still there, untouched. One extra slot per element, and the
"current minimum" is always sitting on the top.

## What this is NOT

| Misconception                                    | Truth                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "A stack is basically an array with extra steps" | Arrays allow random access; a stack deliberately exposes only its top. The discipline is the point.                 |
| "Stacks and queues are interchangeable"          | A stack is LIFO — nearest-first; a queue is FIFO — oldest-first. Flipping one for the other changes the answer.     |
| "A stack is always the fastest data structure"   | Its O(1)s only apply to the two ends; if you need "the oldest" (a queue) or "the middle", reach for something else. |
| "Min Stack just stores a min variable"           | One variable breaks on pop; correctness needs the minimum recorded per level so history survives removal.           |

## Why this matters for the rest of the trip

The stack is the operational heart of recursion — every recursive call pushes a frame you cannot
return past until it pops, which is why "can this be a stack?" is a question to ask before writing a
recursive function. Queues (the FIFO sibling) and deques show up in the BFS-family problems later,
and monotonic stack variations appear in the harder "nearest greater/lesser element" problems. Nail
the pairing-of-the-top habit now and those later days get dramatically cheaper.
