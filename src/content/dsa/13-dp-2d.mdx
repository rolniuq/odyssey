## The one question this page answers

Yesterday the state needed one dimension, so the table was a line. What happens when the answer
depends on _two_ growing things — a position on a grid, or two strings being compared? The one line
becomes a table with two axes, but the philosophy is unchanged: every cell answers a smaller version
of the question, and its answer is built from neighbors that are already settled. This page shows
two canonical 2D states: the grid itself, and a pair of prefixes.

## Start with an analogy

Picture an ant in the corner of a tiled floor. Each step is either south or east, and you want to
count every route to the far corner. There is no way to count routes by looking at the corner alone
— the answer lives in the whole floor: every tile holds "how many ways to get to _me_", each tile
adding the ways of the tile above and the tile to its left.

Now hold two strings, "abcde" and "ace", as two bead necklaces. Ask: how long a chain of beads can I
pull, matching bead colors in the same order in both necklaces? The state again needs two numbers:
how deep into necklace A I have committed, and how deep into necklace B. Two prefixes, two axes —
and the answer again collects in the far corner of a table.

## The 2D state, and its compass

In both problems the cell `dp[i][j]` means "the answer to the subproblem defined by prefix `i` on
the first dimension and prefix `j` on the second." Every transition reads cells that are smaller in
**at least one** dimension — above (`i-1, j`), left (`i, j-1`), or the diagonal (`i-1, j-1`) — never
a larger one. That dependency compass is what makes the fill order (row by row, left to right)
automatic and correct.

## Unique Paths: the grid that counts itself

For Unique Paths, `dp[i][j]` = number of routes from the corner to cell `(i, j)`.

- **Base** — row 0 and column 0 are all 1: from the corner, one route reaches each by going
  straight.
- **Transition** — `dp[i][j] = dp[i-1][j] + dp[i][j-1]`: every route's last step came from above or
  from the left.
- **Answer** — `dp[m-1][n-1]`.

The visualizer fills a 3×3 grid — watch the base row and column light up, then the interior fan out
to `6`. Scale the same recurrence to 3×7 and you get **28**: the recurrence never changes, only the
table size.

## LCS: matching or dropping

For Longest Common Subsequence, `dp[i][j]` = length of the LCS of `text1[0..i)` and `text2[0..j)`.

- **Base** — row 0 and column 0 are zeros: an empty prefix shares nothing with anything.
- **Transition** — compare the final characters of the two prefixes:
  - **Match** (`text1[i-1] === text2[j-1]`): `dp[i][j] = dp[i-1][j-1] + 1` — the matched pair rides
    on the best answer one shorter on both sides.
  - **Mismatch**: `dp[i][j] = max(dp[i-1][j], dp[i][j-1])` — the answer comes from dropping either
    end character.
- **Answer** — `dp[text1.length][text2.length]`.

Trace `"abcde"` vs `"ace"` by hand: the diagonal matches across `a`, `c`, `e` carry a small
`1, 2, 3` on the diagonal, and every mismatch copies the larger neighbor. The corner reads `3`. Cost
is **O(m · n)** time and space; with `m` and `n` the two lengths.

## What this is NOT

| Misconception                                    | Truth                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "A 2D table is a different algorithm from 1D DP" | Same three ingredients, one more axis. The recurrence reads up/left/diagonal instead of one neighbor; the discipline is identical.           |
| "The grid IS the input grid"                     | The DP grid stores _answers_, not the picture. Unique Paths and Flood Fill both deal with grids, but only DP keeps counts in its cells.      |
| "LCS matches greedily at the first equal char"   | A local match may wreck a better future chain. The recurrence evaluates match-or-drop for every pair, never committing blindly.              |
| "The corner is always the maximum cell"          | It is the answer only because the state was defined to make it so — with smaller states _inside_ the table, the corner is the whole problem. |

## Why this matters for the rest of the trip

Two-dimensional states are the parent of a whole family: edit distance, longest palindromic
subsequence, the knapsack with capacity as a second axis, and grid-based graph problems you will
meet when graphs arrive. The mental move — "what two things are growing, and how does one cell
depend on its smaller neighbors?" — generalizes farther than any single recurrence on this page.
